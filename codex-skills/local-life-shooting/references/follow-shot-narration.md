# Third-Party Follow-Shot Narration

Create a natural short video from footage that happened first and narration added afterwards. Treat the filmed person as the protagonist; treat the recorder as a warm, observant supporting voice—the comic straight man (捧哏), not a second protagonist.

## Core rule

Do not turn raw footage into a pre-written dialogue scene. Keep only believable on-site speech for the protagonist. Add the recorder's words as post-production narration, marked `【后期旁白｜记录人】`.

### Sound hierarchy and rhythm

Treat the protagonist's usable on-site speech as the primary sound asset. Select it deliberately; do not retain every conversation.

Keep an on-site line only when it does at least one of these jobs:

- Moves the main event forward: a decision, problem, request, promise, or result.
- Reveals a relationship with a customer, colleague, or family member.
- Shows the protagonist's character, competence, or contradiction more vividly than narration could.
- Creates a naturally occurring reaction, joke, or callback.

Remove greetings, repeated explanations, unclear chatter, privacy-sensitive content, and lines that do not change the story. Preserve the original context around a selected line long enough for it to land; do not cut it into an unnatural sound bite.

Use narration in gaps, before a key on-site line to set it up, or after it to react and reframe it. Do not make narration continuous: alternate `picture + natural sound`, `key protagonist line`, and `recorder narration` so the video can breathe. If no usable protagonist speech exists in the selected material, use continuous but varied recorder narration, leaving brief pauses for action sounds.

The narration has four jobs:

1. Supply context viewers cannot see or hear.
2. Notice a contradiction between what the protagonist says and does.
3. Tease gently, ask the question the audience would ask, or set up a small punchline.
4. Connect clips into one clear event and land a human truth at the end.

## Workflow

### 1. Review the raw material

Identify, with timestamps when possible:

- Who is the protagonist and what are they trying to do?
- What changed, resisted, surprised, or took longer than expected?
- Which on-site lines, actions, expressions, and objects are most revealing?
- Which on-site lines qualify as a story beat, and which should be cut despite being audible?
- Which footage proves the outcome: before/after, decision/result, promise/action, problem/solution, or reaction?

Do not invent events, client claims, dialogue, or outcomes not supported by the material. If an audio line is unclear, flag it rather than fabricating it.

### 2. Find one story spine

Choose one main event, not a chronological diary. Prefer a spine such as:

`A casual errand -> an unexpected complication -> protagonist's characteristic response -> visible result -> callback`

Examples of usable conflicts: “just taking a look” becomes an hour of careful work; a customer changes their mind; an ordinary job reveals meticulousness; a small mishap exposes a warm personality.

Discard clips that do not advance the event, reveal character, create rhythm, or prove the result.

### 3. Select the edit beats

For a 45–60 second video, choose 6–10 clips. Aim for this rhythm:

- 0–4s: visual hook or unusual situation; narration establishes the premise.
- 4–15s: show the protagonist entering or reacting; introduce the tension, ideally with the first useful real line.
- 15–35s: alternate actions, one or two real on-site lines, and only the narration needed to connect them.
- 35–48s: show the proof, outcome, or emotional turn.
- 48–60s: use a short callback, an on-site reaction, or an understated narrated close.

Keep one visual idea per beat. Use hard cuts and natural sound where useful; do not add ornate transitions merely to hide a weak story.

### 4. Write post-production narration

Write as someone who was there, speaking after the fact. Make it sound spontaneous and specific.

Good patterns:

- `本来只是……，结果……`
- `他说……，但你看他……`
- `我当时就知道……`
- `我没看出来，他已经……`
- `嘴上……，手上……`
- `原来他说的“顺路/看一眼/一会儿”，是这个意思。`

Use concise lines, usually 8–20 Chinese characters each. Let visual evidence carry the claim. Teasing must be affectionate and never undermine the protagonist's competence, dignity, or a client relationship.

Avoid ad slogans, omniscient explanations, forced jokes, and narration that merely describes what is already obvious on screen.

### 5. Build the delivery

Default output:

| Time | Selected footage | Audio type | Script | Narrative function |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Use `【现场原声｜主角】` only for speech plausibly captured in the moment. Use `【后期旁白｜记录人】` for every added line. Then provide:

- **主线一句话**: the event and character insight.
- **删减建议**: clips to omit and why, if applicable.
- **旁白录制提示**: conversational, half a beat behind the action; do not read like an advertisement.

If only a rough concept is supplied, first request or propose a realistic shot list, then draft a provisional script clearly labeled as a simulation.

## Quality check

Before delivering, confirm:

- Does the story have a single event rather than a loose workday montage?
- Is the protagonist still the focus in most of the runtime?
- Are the retained on-site lines genuinely useful, and is unnecessary dialogue removed?
- Does the sound alternate naturally between on-site speech, action sound, and narration rather than becoming wall-to-wall voice-over?
- Does every narration line add context, contrast, humor, or a transition?
- Is the final line a payoff or callback rather than an abrupt sales pitch?
- Could the spoken material plausibly have existed before the edit?
